# Topological Spaces and Continuous Functions
## Topological Spaces
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Topology)}`$
	$`\text{A topology on a set X: collection $\mathcal{T}$ of subsets of X s.t.}`$
	$`\text{(1) $\emptyset$ and $X$ are in $\mathcal{T}$ (trivial topology)}`$
	$`\text{(2) The arbitrary union of elements of $\mathcal{T}$ is in $\mathcal{T}$}`$
	$`\text{(3) The finite intersection of elements of}`$ $`\mathcal{T}`$ $`\text{is in}`$ $`\mathcal{T}`$
</callout>
$`U`$ of $`X`$ is an **open set**  of $`X`$ if $`U\in \mathcal{T}`$
The collection of all subsets of $`X`$: **discrete topology**
$`\mathcal{T}_f = \{ U \mid X-U  \ \text{is finite or X} \}`$: **finite complement topology**
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def.}`$
	$`\text{If } \mathcal{T}'\supset \mathcal{T} \text{, then $\mathcal{T'}$ is \textbf{finer} than $\mathcal{T}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ is \textbf{coarser} than $\mathcal{T'}$}`$
</callout>
$`\mathcal{T}`$ is **comparable **with** **$`\mathcal{T'}`$** **if $`\mathcal{T} \supset \mathcal{T'}`$ or $`\mathcal{T'} \supset \mathcal{T}`$
** **
## Basis for a Topology
For describing topology $`\mathcal{T}`$, we can specify the entire collection $`\mathcal{T}`$ of open sets.
But this is too difficult, so instead specify a smaller collection of subsets of $`X`$ and define the topology in terms of that.
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Basis)}`$
	$`\text{A \textbf{basis} for a topology on $\mathcal{T}$ is a collection $\mathcal{B}$ of subsets of X s.t.}`$
	$`\text{(1) $\forall x \in X$, there is at least one basis element B$\ni x$}`$
	$`\text{(2) If $x \in B_1 \cap B_2$, then $\exist B_3 \ni x $ s.t. $B_3 \subset B_1 \cap B_2$}`$
</callout>
<empty-block/>
### Topology generated by Basis
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`U \subset X \text{ is open in X }`$
	$`\text{if   $\forall x \in X, \exist B \subset \mathcal{B}$ s.t. $x\in B \subset U$}`$
</callout>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Lemma 13.1. $\mathcal{T}=\{ \cup_\alpha U_{\alpha}  \}$}`$ 
</callout>
This expression of $`U`$ is **not** **unique**
(cf. In linear algebra, a linear combination of basis vectors is unique)
<empty-block/>
### Basis for a given Topology
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Lemma 13.2. Suppose $\mathcal{B}$ is a collection of open sets of X}`$
	$`\text{If $\forall U$ of X and $\forall x \in U, \exist B \in \mathcal{B}$ s.t. $x\in B \subset U$}`$
	$`\text{Then $\mathcal{B}$ is a bais for the topology of X}`$
</callout>
<empty-block/>
We can use bases to have a criterion for determining whether one is finer than another
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Lemma 13.3. }`$
	$`\mathcal{T} \text{ is finer than } \mathcal{T'} \iff \text{for each $x \in X$ and each $B\in \mathcal{B}$ containing $x, \exist B' \in \mathcal{B'}$ s.t. $x \in B' \subset B$}`$
</callout>
<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
	($`\Leftarrow`$) Let $`x \in U`$ of $`\mathcal{T}`$. Since $`\mathcal{B}`$ generates $`\mathcal{T}`$, there is a $`B \in \mathcal{B}`$ s.t. $`x \in B \subset U`$. By assumption, there is a $`B'`$ s.t. $`x \in B' \subset B`$, so $`x \in B' \subset U`$.
	($`\Rightarrow`$) Since $`B \in \mathcal{T} \subset \mathcal{T'},\text{ so } B\in \mathcal{T'}`$. $`\mathcal{T'}`$ is generated by $`\mathcal{B'}`$, there is a $`B' \in \mathcal{B'} \text{ s.t. } x \in B' \subset B`$
</details>
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def.}`$
	$`\text{\textbf{standard topology}: generated by basis (a,b)}`$
	$`\text{\textbf{lower limit topology}: generated by basis [a,b)}`$
	$`\text{\textbf{K-topology}: generated by basis (a,b) along with all sets of the form (a,b)-K}`$
</callout>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Lemma 13.4.}`$
	$`\text{$\mathbb{R}_l$ and $\mathbb{R}_K$ are strictly finer than standard topology, but are not comparable}`$
</callout>
<empty-block/>
If start with a given collection of sets taking finite intersections?
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Subbasis)}`$
	$`\text{A \textbf{subbasis} $\mathcal{S}$ for a topology on X: collection of subsets of X whose union }`$
	$`\text{equals X. The \textbf{topology generated by the subbasis} is all unions of finite}`$
	$`\text{intersections of elemtns of $\mathcal{S}$}`$
</callout>

## The Order Topology
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Order Topology)}`$
	$`\text{Let $\mathcal{B}$ be the collection of all sets of the following types:}`$
	$`\text{(1) (a,b)}`$
	$`\text{(2) [$a_0$, b) where $a_0$ is the smallest element in X}`$
	$`\text{(3) (a, $b_0$] where $b_0$ is the largest element in X}`$
	$`\text{The topology generated by this basis is the \textbf{order topology}}`$
</callout>

## The Product Topology on X × Y
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Product Topology)}`$
	$`\text{The \textbf{product topology} on X $\times$ Y is the topology having basis $\mathcal{B}$ of all sets}`$
	$`\text{of the form U $\times$ V, where U is open in X and V is open in Y}`$
</callout>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 15.1.}`$
	$`\text{$\mathcal{B}$: basis for X and $\mathcal{C}$: basis for Y}`$
	$`\text{$\mathcal{D} = \{ B \times C \mid B \in \mathcal{B} \text{ and } C \in \mathcal{C} \}$ is a basis for topology on X $\times$ Y}`$ 
</callout>
<empty-block/>
It is useful to express the product topology in terms of a subbasis. First, we define projections.
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Projections)}`$
	$`\pi_1:X\times Y \rightarrow X, \ \pi_1(x,\ y) = x`$
	$`\pi_2:X\times Y \rightarrow Y, \ \pi_2(x,\ y) = y`$
	$`\text{The maps are called \textbf{projections} of X $\times$ Y onto 1st and 2nd factors}`$
</callout>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 15.2.}`$
	$`\text{$\mathcal{S} = \{ \pi_1^{-1}(U) \mid U \text{ open in X} \} \cup \{ \pi_2^{-1}(V) \mid \text{V open in Y} \}$ is a subbasis for X $\times$ Y}`$
</callout>
<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
	Let $`\mathcal{T}`$: product topology on $`X \times Y`$, $`\mathcal{T'}`$: topology generated by $`\mathcal{S}`$. Since every element of $`\mathcal{S}`$ in $`\mathcal{T}`$, so do arbitrary unons of finite intersections of elements of $`\mathcal{S}`$, thus $`\mathcal{T'} \subset \mathcal{T}`$. 
	Conversely, every basis $`U \times V`$ of $`\mathcal{T}`$, $`U \times V = \pi_1^{-1}(U) \cap \pi_2^{-1}(V)`$, so $`U \times V \in \mathcal{T'}`$, so that $`\mathcal{T} \subset \mathcal{T'}`$.
</details>
<empty-block/>
## The Subspace Topology
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Subspace Topology)}`$
	$`\text{For topological space (X, $\mathcal{T}$) and $Y \subset X$,}`$
	$`\text{$\mathcal{T}_Y=\{ Y \cap U \mid U \in \mathcal{T} \}$ is a topology on Y (\textbf{subspace topology})}`$
	$`\text{With this topology, Y is called \textbf{subspace} of X}`$
</callout>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Lemma 16.1.}`$
	$`\text{$\mathcal{B}_Y=\{ B \cap Y \mid B \in \mathcal{B} \}$ is a basis for the subspace topology on Y}`$
</callout>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Lemma 16.2.}`$
	$`\text{If U is open in Yand Y is open in X, then U  is open in X}`$
</callout>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 16.3.}`$
	$`\text{Subspace topology $\Rightarrow$ Product topology} \\= \text{Product topology $\Rightarrow$ Subspace topology}`$
</callout>
<empty-block/>
cf) subspace ⇒ order topology ≠ order topology ⇒ subspace topology

**Ordered square**: $`I \times I`$, where $`I=[0,1]`$** **
<empty-block/>
$`Y \subset X`$ is **convex **in** **$`X`$** **if for each pair of points a \< b of $`Y`$, entire (a, b) of points of $`X`$ lies in $`Y`$ ** **
It means that if $`c \in X, a < c < b`$, then $`c \in Y`$
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 16.4.}`$
	$`X\text{: ordered set in order topology, Y$\subset X$ convex in X}`$
	$`\text{Then the order topology on Y = topology Y inherits as a subspace of Y}`$
</callout>
<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
	$`\text{(sub $\subset$order)}`$
	Let $`(a, +\infty) \in X`$. We need intersection with Y. 
	First $`a \in Y`$, $`(a,+\infty) \cap Y = \{ x \mid x \in Y, \ x > a \}`$
	Second $`a \notin Y`$, then a is upper or lower bound of Y, because if not, $`(a-\epsilon, \ a+\epsilon ) \in Y,`$ so $`a \in Y`$, it is contradiction. Thus for lower bound, intersection is Y and for upper bound, intersection is empty.
	Similarly, intersection $`(-\infty, \ a)`$  with $`Y`$ is either an open ray, $`Y`$ itself or empty set.
	Since $`(a, +\infty)\cap Y`$ and $`(-\infty,\ a)\cap Y`$ form the subbasis for the subspace topology on Y, and each is open in order topology.
	$`\text{(order $\subset$sub)}`$
	Since the open rays of $`Y`$ are subbasis for the order topology on $`Y`$
</details>

## Closed Sets and Limit Points
A is **closed **if X-A is open
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 17.1.}`$
	$`\text{(1) $\empty$ and X are closed}`$
	$`\text{(2) Finite unions of closed sets are closed}`$
	$`\text{(3) Arbitrary intersections of closed sets are closed}`$
</callout>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 17.2.}`$
	$$
	\text{A is closed in Y $\iff$ C $\cap$ Y, where C is closed in X}
	$$
</callout>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 17.3. If A is closed in Y and Y is closed in X, then A is closed in X}`$ 
</callout>
<empty-block/>
**Interior** of A ($`\text{Int A}`$): union of all open sets contained in A = largest open set contained in A
**Closure** of A ($`\bar{A}`$): intersection of all closed sets containing A = smallest closed set containing A
$$
\text{If A is open, then A=Int A and if A is closed, then A = $\bar{A}$}
$$
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 17.4. $A\subset Y$. $\bar{A}$: closure of A in X. \\Then the closure of A in Y = $\bar{A}\cap Y$}`$
</callout>
<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
	Let B: closure of A in Y. $`\bar{A}`$  is closed in X, so $`\bar{A} \cap Y`$ is closed in Y.
	Since $`\bar{A} \cap Y`$ contains A and B is smallest closed set containing A, so $`B \subset \bar{A} \cap Y`$
	B is closed in Y, so $`B = C\cap Y`$ for some closed C in X. Then C contains A, because $`\bar{A}`$ is smallest closed set containing A, so $`\bar{A} \subset C`$. Thus $`\bar{A}\cap Y \subset C\cap Y = B`$
</details>
<empty-block/>
But the collection of closed sets are too big, so we need another way to describe the closure
A **intersects** B if $`A \cap B \neq \empty`$
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 17.5. A $\subset$ X}`$
	$`\text{(1) $x \in \bar{A} \iff \forall$open $ U \ni x$ intersets A}`$
	$`\text{(2) $x \in \bar{A} \iff \forall$basis $ B \ni x$ intersets A}`$
</callout>
U is a **neighborhood** of x: U is an open set containing x
Then $`\text{(1): $x \in \bar{A} \iff \forall$nbhd of x intersets A}`$
<empty-block/>
### Limit Points
There is another way to describe closure
x is a **limit point** of A if every neighborhood of x intersects A other than x itself
↔ if it belongs to closure of A-\{x\}
⇒ x may lie in A or not.
<empty-block/>
ex) In R, A = (0, 1\], then limit points of A = \[0, 1\]
<empty-block/>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 17.6. A$'$: set of all limit points of A. Then $\bar{A}=A \cup A'$}`$
</callout>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Cor 17.7. closed $\iff$ it contains all limit points}`$
</callout>

(2) sequence converges at most one point
But in general topological space, it is not true

	$`\text{Def. (Hausdorff Space)}`$
	$`\text{A topological space X is called \textbf{Hausdorff space} if for each distinct pair }`$$`\text{$x_1,\ x_2$, there exist neighborhoods $U_1 $ and $U_2$ of $x_1$ and $x_2$ are disjoint}`$
</callout>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 17.8. Every finite point set in Hausdorff space X is closed}`$
</callout>

**T1-axiom**: the finite point sets be closed
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 17.9. X satisfying $T_1$ axiom}`$
	$`\text{x is a limit point of A }`$
	$`\text{$\iff$ every neighborhood of x contains infinitely many points of A}`$
</callout>
<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
	(⇐) If every neighborhood of x intersects A in infinitely many points, then it certainly intersects A with some point other than x.
	(⇒) Suppose x is a limit point of A and some neighborhood U of x intersects A only in finitely many points of A. Then U intersects A-\{x\} in finitely many points  → $`\{ x_1, ... \ ,x_m  \}`$ be the points of $`U \cap (A-\{x\})`$. Then $`X-\{ x_1, ... \ ,x_m  \}`$ is an open in X since finie point set is closed in T1 axiom. Then $`U\cap(X- \{ x_1, ... \ ,x_m  \})`$ is a  neighborhood of x does not intersect A-\{x\}.
	It is contradiction.
</details>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 17.10. X is a Hausdorff space.  }`$
	$`\text{Then a sequence of points converges to at most one point of X}`$
</callout>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 17.11.}`$
	$`\text{(1) Every orderd set is a Hausdorff space in the order topology}`$
	$`\text{(2) The product of two Hausdorff spaces is a Hausdorff space}`$
	$`\text{(3) A subspace of a Hausdorff space is  a Hausdorff space}`$
</callout>

## Continuous Functions
A function $`f:X\rightarrow Y`$ is **continuous **if for each open subset V of Y, the set $`f^{-1}(V)`$ is open in X
(1) If Y is given by a basis $`\mathcal{B}`$, f is continuous if inverse image of every basis element is open
(2) If Y is given by a subbasis $`\mathcal{S}`$, it is continuous if inverse image of each subbasis element is open
<empty-block/>
ex) $`f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}_l, \ f(x)=x`$. It is not continuous because inverse image of open subset \[a, b) in $`\mathbb{R}_l`$ is not open in $`\mathbb{R}`$. On the other hand, $`g:\mathbb{R}_l \rightarrow \mathbb{R}`$ is continuous.
<empty-block/>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 18.1. TFAE}`$
	$`\text{(1) f is continuous}`$
	$`\text{(2) $f(\bar{A}) \subset \overline{f(A)}$}`$
	$`\text{(3) For every closed set B of Y, $f^{-1}(B)$ is closed in X}`$
	$`\text{(4) For each neighborhood V of f(x), $\exist$ a neighborhood of x s.t. $f(U) \subset V$}`$
</callout>
<empty-block/>
### Homeomorphisms
f is **homeomorphism **if f and inverse of f are continuous
$$
f(U)\text{ is open} \iff \text{U is open}
$$
Homeomorphism preserves topological structures
<empty-block/>
Suppose f is an injective continuous map and Z is the image set f(X). 
Then the function $`f':X\rightarrow Z`$ is bijective. If f’ is a homeomorphism, 
we say f’ is a **topological imbedding** of X in Y
<empty-block/>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 18.2. (Constructing continuous functions)}`$
	$`\text{(1) (Constant function) constant function f is continuous}`$
	$`\text{(2) (Inclusion) If $A \subset X$, then $j:A\rightarrow X$ is continuous}`$
	$`\text{(3) (Composition) $f:X \rightarrow Y, \ g:Y\rightarrow Z$, then $g \circ f: X \rightarrow Z$ is continuous}`$
	$`\text{(4) (Restricting the domain) $f|_A:A\rightarrow Y$ is continuous}`$
	$`\text{(5) (Restricting or expanding the range) }`$
	$`\text{  $\quad \ $      $f:X\rightarrow Z$, where $Z\subset Y$ or $Y \subset Z$ is continuous}`$
	$`\text{(6) (Local formulation) $X=\bigcup U_\alpha$. If $f|U_\alpha$ is continuous, then f is continuous}`$
</callout>
<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
	(6) Let V open in Y, $`f^{-1}(V) \cap U_\alpha = (f|U_\alpha)^{-1}(V)`$. $`f^{-1}(V) = \bigcup(f^{-1}(V) \cap U_\alpha)`$.
</details>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 18.3. (The pasting lemma)  $X=A\cup B$, where A, B is closed in X}`$
	$`\text{Let $f: A \rightarrow Y$ and $g:B \rightarrow Y$ be continuous and f(x)=g(x) if $x \in A\cap B$}`$
	$`\text{Then}\  h(x)= \begin{cases} h(x)=f(x) \text{ if }x\in A \\ h(x) = g(x) \text{ if } x \in B \end{cases} \  \text{  is continuous}`$
</callout>
<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
	Let C be closed in Y. $`h^{-1}(C)=f^{-1}(C) \cup g^{-1}(C)`$ , since f, g are continuous, so inverse of h is closed. Thus h is continuous
</details>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 18.4. (Maps into products)}`$
	$`f:A\rightarrow X\times Y,\ f(a) = (f_1(a),\ f_2(a))`$
	$`\text{f is continuous $\iff$ $f_1$ and $f_2$ are continuous }`$
</callout>
<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
	Note) $`\pi_1,\ \pi_2`$ are continuous projections
	(⇒) $`f_i(a)=\pi_i(f(a))`$, since composition of two continuous function, so $`f_i`$ is continuous
	(⇐) Let $`U \times V`$ be a basis for the topology of $`X \times Y`$
	$`a\in f^{-1}(U\times V)\iff f(a) \in U\times V \iff f_1(a)\in U \text{ and }f_2(a) \in V`$
	Thus $`f^{-1}(U \times V)=f_1^{-1}(U) \cap f_2^{-1}(V)`$ and this is open.
	<empty-block/>
</details>
The maps $`f_1\,\ f_2`$ are **coordinate functions** of f

## The Product Topology
We have only considered the product of two topological spaces $`X \times Y`$. 
In this section, we define a more general cartesian product space
There is two general cases (1) finite $`X_1 \times \cdots \times X_n`$ and (2) infinite $`X_1 \times X_2 \times \cdots`$
<empty-block/>
Before define a topology on the cartesian product, we introduce the notion of the cartesian product to more general
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (J-tuple)}`$
	$`\text{\textbf{J-tuple} of elemtns of X: $\mathbf{x}:J\rightarrow X$ }`$
	$`\text{If $\alpha \in J$, the value of $\mathbf{x}$ at $\alpha$ is $x_\alpha$ rather than $\mathbf{x}(\alpha)$; $\alpha$th \textbf{coordinate} of $\mathbf{x}$}`$
	$$
	\mathbf{x}=(x_\alpha)_{\alpha\in J}
	$$
</callout>
Set of all J-tuples of elements of X = $`X^J`$
ex) J=\{1.2\}, J-tuple $`\mathbf{x}=(x_1\in X,\ x_2 \in X)`$
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Cartesian product)}`$
	$`\text{Let $\{ A_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in J}$ be an indexed family and $X=\bigcup_{\alpha\in J} A_\alpha$}`$
	 $`\text{The \textbf{cartesian product} of indexed family $\displaystyle \prod_{\alpha \in J}A_\alpha$}`$
	$`\text{is defined to be the set of all J-tuples $(x_\alpha)_{\alpha \in J}$ of elements of X s.t. $x_\alpha \in A_\alpha$}`$
	$`\text{That is, \{ $\mathbf{x}:J\rightarrow \displaystyle \bigcup_{\alpha \in J}A_\alpha\mid \mathbf{x}_\alpha \in A_\alpha$ \} }`$ 
</callout>
ex) $`\mathbf{x}=(x_1 \in A_1,\ x_2\in A_2)`$
If $`A_\alpha=X`$, then cartesian product is $`X^J`$
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Box topology)}`$
	$`\text{Let $\{ X_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in J}$ be an indexed family of topological spaces}`$
	$`\text{Take a basis for $\displaystyle \prod_{\alpha \in J}X_\alpha$ as $\displaystyle \prod_{\alpha \in J}U_\alpha$, where $U_\alpha$ is open in $X_\alpha$}`$
	$`\text{The topology generated by this basis is \textbf{box topology}}`$ 
</callout>
<empty-block/>
$$
\pi_\beta:\displaystyle \prod_{\alpha \in J}X_\alpha \rightarrow X_\beta
$$
This is the function assigning each element of the product space its $`\beta`$th coordinate
$$
\pi_\beta((x_\alpha)_{\alpha\in J})=x_\beta
$$
This function is called **projection mapping**
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Product topology)}`$
	$$
	\mathcal{S}_\beta =\{ \pi_\beta^{-1}(U_\beta) \mid U_\beta \text{ open in }X_\beta \}
	$$
	$$
	\mathcal{S}=\displaystyle \bigcup _{\beta \in J} \mathcal{S}_\beta
	$$
	$`\text{Then topology generated by the subbasis $\mathcal{S}$ is \textbf{product topology}}`$
	$`\text{In this topology, $\displaystyle \prod_{\alpha \in J}X_\alpha$ is called \textbf{product space}}`$
</callout>
Consider the basis $`\mathcal{B}`$ generated by $`\mathcal{S}`$
When we intersect elements of $`\mathcal{S}_\beta`$, because $`\pi_\beta^{-1}(U_\beta)\cap \pi_\beta^{-1}(V_\beta)=\pi_\beta^{-1}(U_\beta\cap V_\beta)\in \mathcal{S}_\beta`$, we get something new if intersect elements from different $`\mathcal{S}_\beta`$
So, we can describe the element of basis as follows
$$
B=\pi_{\beta_1}^{-1}(U_{\beta_1})\cap \pi_{\beta_2}^{-1}(U_{\beta_2}) \ \cap \cdots \cap \pi_{\beta_n}^{-1}(U_{\beta_n}) 
$$
$$
\text{$B=\displaystyle \prod_{\alpha \in J}U_\alpha$, where $U_\alpha=X_\alpha$ if $\alpha \neq \beta_1, \cdots , \beta_n$}
$$
The finite products, the two topologies are the same
The box topology is generally finer than the product topology
Since important theorems about finite products will also hold for arbitrary products if using the product topology but not box topology, we prefer the product topology
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 19.2.}`$
	$`\text{$\displaystyle \prod_{\alpha \in J}B_\alpha$ is basis for box topology and the same form where $B_\alpha$ for finitely many} \\ \text{$\alpha$ and $B_\alpha =X_\alpha$ for all the remaining $\alpha$ is a basis for the product topology}`$
</callout>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 19.3. Let $A_\alpha$ be a subspace of $X_\alpha$. }`$
	$`\text{Then $\displaystyle \prod_{\alpha \in J}A_\alpha$ is a subspace of $\displaystyle \prod_{\alpha \in J}X_\alpha$ in both topologies}`$
</callout>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 19.4. }`$
	$`\text{If $X_\alpha$ is a Hausdoff space, then$\displaystyle \prod_{\alpha \in J}X_\alpha$ is a  Hausdorff space in both topologies}`$
</callout>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 19.5. Let $A_\alpha \subset X_\alpha$.  For both topologies, }`$
	$$
	\displaystyle \prod_{\alpha \in J}\bar{A}_\alpha=\overline{\displaystyle \prod_{\alpha \in J}A_\alpha}
	$$
</callout>
<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
	$`(\subset)`$Let $`\mathbf{x}=(x_\alpha) \in \prod\bar{A}_\alpha`$. WTS: $`\mathbf{x} \in \overline{\prod A_\alpha}`$. Let $`U=\prod U_\alpha`$ be a basis element containing $`\mathbf{x}`$
	Since $`x_\alpha \in \bar{A}_\alpha`$, $`\exist y_\alpha \in U_\alpha \cap A_\alpha \neq \empty`$. Then $`\mathbf{y}=(y_\alpha) \in U \cap \prod A_\alpha`$, so $`\mathbf{x} \in \overline{\prod A_\alpha}`$
	$`(\supset)`$Let $`\mathbf{x} \in \overline{\prod A_\alpha}`$ and $`V_\beta`$ be an arbitrary open set containing $`x_\beta`$. Since $`\pi_\beta^{-1}(V_\beta)`$ is open in $`\prod X_\alpha`$, $`\mathbf{y} \in \pi_\beta^{-1}(V_\beta) \cap \prod A_\alpha`$. Then $`y_\beta \in V_\beta \cap A_\beta`$, thus $`x_\beta \in \bar{A}_\beta`$
</details>
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 19.6. Let $f:A\rightarrow \prod X_\alpha$, $f(a)=(f_\alpha(a))_{\alpha \in J}$ where $f_\alpha: A\rightarrow X_\alpha$}`$
	$$
	\text{Then f is continuous $\iff$ $f_\alpha$ is continuous}
	$$
</callout>
<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
	(⇒) $`f_\beta=\pi_\beta \circ f`$; composition of two continuous functions, it is continuous
	(⇐) It suffices to show that the inverse of subbasis element is open in A.
	$`f^{-1}(\pi_\beta^{-1}(U_\beta))=f_\beta^{-1}(U_\beta)`$, since $`f_\beta`$ is continuous, it is open.
</details>

## The Metric Topology
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Metric)}`$
	$`\text{A \textbf{metric} on a set X: $d:X\times X \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ s.t.}`$
	$`\text{(1) d(x, y) $\geq 0$}`$
	$`\text{(2) d(x, y) = d(y, x)}`$
	$`\text{(3) (Triangle inequality) d(x, y) + d(y, z) $\geq$ d(x, z)}`$
</callout>
d(x, y) is called **distance **between x and y in metric d
$$
B_d(x, \epsilon)=\{y \mid d(x, y) < \epsilon \}
$$
The set of all points whose distance from x is less than $`\epsilon`$: $`\textbf{$\epsilon$-ball}`$ centered at x
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Metric topology)}`$
	$`\text{The collection of all $\epsilon$-balls is a basis for topology on X: \textbf{metric topology}}`$
</callout>
<details>
<summary>Why it is a basis?</summary>
	The 1st condition is trivial
	Before 2nd condition, Claim: $`\text{If $y \in B_d(x,\epsilon)$, then $\exist B_d(y, \delta) \subset B_d(x, \epsilon)$}`$
	Let $`\delta = \epsilon -d(x,y)`$, since for $`z \in B_d(y,\delta),\ d(y,z) < \epsilon-d(x,y) \Rightarrow d(x,z) \leq d(x,y)+d(y,z) < \epsilon`$, $`B_d(y,\delta) \subset B_d(x,\epsilon)`$
	For 2nd condition of basis, let $`y \in B_1 \cap B_2`$. Then by above, $`B(y, \delta_1) \subset B_1,\ B(y, \delta_2 ) \subset B_2`$
	Let $`\delta = \min(\delta_1, \ \delta_2)`$, then $`B(y, \delta) \subset B_1 \cap B_2`$
</details>
We can rephrase the definition of the metric topology
$$
U \in \mathcal{T}_{\text{metric}} \iff \forall y \in U,\ \exist \delta>0 \text{ s.t. } B_d(y,\epsilon) \subset U
$$
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Metrizable)}`$
	$`\text{X is \textbf{metrizable} if $\exist$ metric d that induces the topology on X (\textbf{metric space})}`$
</callout>
<empty-block/>
Metrizability of a space depends only on the topology of the space, but properties do not
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Bounded)}`$
	$`A\subset X \text{ is \textbf{bounded} if $\exist$ M s.t.  $d(a_1, a_2) \leq $M}`$
	$`\text{If A is bounded, then the \textbf{diameter} of A, diam A$= \sup\{d(a_1,a_2) \mid a_1,\ a_2 \in A\}$}`$
</callout>
Boundedness of a set depends metric d

	metric is trivial
	The collection of $`\epsilon`$-balls with $`\epsilon<1`$ under these two metrices are the same

	$$

	$$

	$$

<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 20.3. }`$
	$`\text{The topology generated by euclidean metric d and square metric $\rho$ are the }`$
	$`\text{same as the product topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$}`$
</callout>

<summary>Proof</summary>

	\rho(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})\leq d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) \leq \sqrt{n}\rho(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})

## The Quotient Topology
The concept of **quotient topology** is essentially the formal mathematical language for **"gluing" **shapes together. In geometry, the intuitive process of **"cut and paste"**—such as taking a flat sheet of paper and taping its edges to create a cylinder or a torus—allows us to build complex structures from simpler ones
![](notion-file-block://db70ad69-a5f8-8270-81e2-01ec5f7739de/b620bd05-fb79-466d-b189-6cc16f363988?space_id=a572f2a2-f7b0-4923-b5c0-74f5db67bcd1&name=image.png)
![](notion-file-block://a8a0ad69-a5f8-83ec-ba22-01f543899bb6/f2f017de-27b4-4efe-af01-fc6faa20cb72?space_id=a572f2a2-f7b0-4923-b5c0-74f5db67bcd1&name=image.png)
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Quotient map)}`$
	$`\text{Let $p: X \rightarrow Y$ be a surjective map}`$
	$`\text{The map $p$ is \textbf{quotient map} when $U \subset Y \text{ is open}\iff p^{-1}(U) \text{ is open}$}`$
</callout>
The quotient topology defines a subset $`U \subseteq Y`$ as open if and only if its preimage $`p^{-1}(U)`$ is open 
in the original space $`X`$. Geometrically, this serves as a mathematical mechanism for **"gluing"** 
points together; when two distant points in $`X`$ are merged into a single point in $`Y`$, the new 
neighborhood is considered "open" only if the surroundings of both original points were open. This 
ensures that the newly formed boundary is seamlessly connected, allowing for a smooth 
topological flow across the seam where the edges were physically joined.
<empty-block/>
An equivalent condition is $`A \subset Y \text{ is closed}\iff p^{-1}(A) \text{ is closed}`$<br>C of X is **saturated **if C contains every set $`p^{-1}({y})`$ that it intersects
It implies that $`C=p^{-1}(p(C))`$<br>
For quotient map p, 
we can say p is <span underline="true">continuous</span> and p maps <span underline="true">saturated open(closed) sets of X</span> to open(closed) sets of Y
<br>Also, <span underline="true">surjective continuous open(closed)</span> map is a quotient map
But quotient map is neither open nor closed
ex) 
$$
p(x)=\begin{cases} 
x & \text{for } x \in [0,1] \\
x -1 & \text{for } x \in [2,3] 
\end{cases}
$$
It is a quotient map, but not open map
<empty-block/>
<callout icon="📘" color="blue_bg">
	$`\text{Def. (Quotient topology)}`$
	$`\text{For surjective map $p:X\rightarrow A$, there exists one topology(\textbf{quotient}) $\mathcal{T}$ on A}`$
	$`\text{relative to which p is a quotient map}`$
</callout>
ex)
p: R → A = \{a, b, c\}
$$
p(x)=\begin{cases} a &\text{ if }  x>0    \\ b &\text{ if } x < 0 \\ c &\text{ if } x = 0\end{cases}

On quotient map, in general, subspaces do not behave well
<callout icon="📜" color="yellow_bg">
	$`\text{Thm 22.1.}`$
	$`\text{Let p be a quotient map; let A be a subspace of X saturated with respect to p}`$
	$`\text{Let $q:A \rightarrow P(A)$}`$
	$`\text{(1) If A is either open or closed in X, then q is a quotient map}`$
	$`\text{(2) If p is either open or closed map, then q is a quotient map}`$
</callout>
<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
	(step 1) 
	$$
	\begin{aligned}
q^{-1}(V) = p^{-1}(V) & \hspace{2em} \text{if } V \subset p(A) \\
p(U \cap A) = p(U) \cap p(A) & \hspace{2em} \text{if } U \subset X
\end{aligned}
	$$
	Since $`V \subset p(A)`$ and A is saturated, so $`p^{-1}(V)`$ is contained in A
	Because in A, q = p, so $`q^{-1}(V) = p^{-1}(V)`$
	$`p(U \cap A) \subset p(U) \cap p(A)`$ is trivial. Let y = p(u), then $`u \in p^{-1}(y) \subset A`$, so 
	$`u \in U \cap A`$
	<empty-block/>
	(step 2)
	Given V of p(A), WTS: $`q^{-1}(V)`$ is open in A then V is open in p(A)
	(1) A is open
	Since $`q^{-1}(V)`$ is open in A and A is open in X, so $`q^{-1}(V)`$ is open in X
	For $`V \subset p(A)`$, $`q^{-1}(V) = p^{-1}(V)`$, so $`p^{-1}(V)`$ is open in X
	Thus V is open in Y because p is quotient map, in particular, V is open in p(A)
	(2) p is open map
	Since $`q^{-1}(V) = p^{-1}(V)`$ and $`q^{-1}(V)`$ is open in A, so $`p^{-1}(V)=U \cap A`$ for some
	open U in X. Because p is surjective, $`p(p^{-1}(V))=V`$
	Thus $`V= p(p^{-1}(V)) = p(U\cap A)=p(U) \cap p(A)`$, it implies that p(U) is open in p(A)
	<empty-block/>
	(step 3) for closed, similar to step 2
	<empty-block/>
</details>
Composite of two quotient maps is quotient map
However, products of maps do not behave well
If both maps are open maps, then product space is a quotient
If X is Hausdorff, there is no reason to satisfy X\* is Hausdorff
